Things School Doesn't Teach You About Programming

8/26/26

Programming classes are useful.

I should probably start with that before this entire post sounds like I'm declaring war on computer science teachers.

School can teach you variables, loops, functions, arrays, classes, algorithms, and whatever else is on the curriculum. That's important. You need to learn what the tools actually are before you can do much with them.

But after spending years programming and making games, I've realized there's a massive difference between knowing how programming works and knowing how to actually make something with it.

Because nobody warns you about the stupid shit.

Nobody prepares you for spending two hours debugging something only to discover you wrote false instead of true.

Nobody teaches you the ancient programming technique of:

“I have absolutely no idea why this works, but removing it breaks everything, so we're keeping it forever.”

So here's some stuff I've learned from actually making games that programming classes don't really prepare you for.

1. Most Programming Is Not Programming

When I first started programming, I imagined programming as constantly writing code.

You sit down.

You know what you need.

You write the code.

It works.

You are a programmer now.

This is a lie.

Sometimes programming is writing code.

Sometimes programming is staring at the same 14 lines for twenty minutes trying to figure out why they aren't working.

Sometimes it's reading documentation.

Sometimes it's opening a script you wrote six months ago and thinking:

Who wrote this garbage?

Then realizing:

Oh. Me.

Sometimes it's changing one number from 0.2 to 0.3, launching the game, deciding it feels worse, changing it to 0.25, launching the game, changing it to 0.27, launching the game—

Congratulations.

You have just spent thirty minutes programming approximately nothing.

And somehow the game is better now.

2. Getting Something to Work Is Only Step One

This is probably one of the biggest things I've learned making games.

You make a door.

Player presses E.

Door opens.

HOLY SHIT.

IT WORKS.

You're done!

Except you're not.

What if the player presses E seventeen times?

What if they walk away while it's opening?

What if there's another door beside it?

What if the player dies while interacting with it?

What if the animation gets interrupted?

What if the door is already open?

What if the player manages to stand inside the fucking door?

Making something work under the exact circumstances you imagined is usually pretty easy.

Making something work when the player behaves like an escaped zoo animal is considerably harder.

And players will behave like escaped zoo animals.

If there's a button they're only supposed to press once, somebody will press it 400 times.

If there's a wall they aren't supposed to climb, somebody will spend twenty minutes climbing it.

If there's a tiny gap between two props that obviously leads nowhere, somebody will immediately think:

secret passage.

And then they'll get stuck.

And then it's your problem.

3. You Will Never Predict How People Play Your Game

This is related to the last one, but it's important enough to get its own section.

When you're developing something, you know how everything works.

You know the green light means the door is unlocked.

You know you're supposed to go left.

You know that machine is decoration.

You know the giant flashing button is important.

Then you give the game to somebody else.

They walk into the room.

They look directly at the giant flashing button.

They turn around.

They spend five minutes inspecting a trash can.

WHAT ARE YOU DOING???

But you can't blame them.

Well.

You can.

It's very fun.

But ultimately, if several players don't understand something, that's probably telling you something about the game.

Making games has taught me that something being obvious to the developer means absolutely nothing.

Of course it's obvious to me.

I MADE THE FUCKING THING.

4. Debugging Is Basically Detective Work

School usually gives you problems where there's an intended solution.

Real projects give you shit like:

“Sometimes, if the player enters this room while holding an item after previously dying, the lights don't turn on.”

Cool.

Why?

No idea.

Have fun.

Then you start investigating.

Is the trigger broken?

No.

Is the light broken?

No.

Is the room loading incorrectly?

No.

Is the save system doing something weird?

Maybe.

Is some completely unrelated script from four months ago setting a variable it has absolutely no business touching?

THERE IT IS.

Debugging isn't always knowing the solution.

A lot of the time, it's slowly proving what isn't causing the problem until only one stupid possibility remains.

And the satisfaction when you finally find it?

Amazing.

The satisfaction when the fix creates two new bugs?

Less amazing.

5. Your Old Code Will Become An Ancient Civilization

When you're writing code, everything makes perfect sense.

Obviously thing2 means that.

Obviously this random boolean controls the door.

Obviously this function needs to run here.

Why would I need comments?

I understand it perfectly.

Then six months pass.

You open the script.

if thing2 && !thing3: doThing()

What is thing2?

What is thing3?

What is the thing we're doing?

Why does deleting this line cause the ceiling to disappear?

Nobody knows.

The civilization that understood this technology is gone.

All that remains are ruins.

And unfortunately, you are the archaeologist.

6. Temporary Solutions Are The Most Permanent Objects In The Universe

Every programmer has said:

“I'll clean this up later.”

No you won't.

That variable is going to survive until release.

That placeholder object is going to become infrastructure.

That script you made in five minutes because you needed to test something is eventually going to control seventeen systems and possibly the national power grid.

This is especially dangerous in game development because you prototype constantly.

You think:

I'll just make a quick system to see if this idea works.

The idea works.

Great!

Now you build something else on top of it.

Then something else.

Then another system depends on it.

Three months later you realize your entire game depends on a script called:

testthing.cs

You cannot remove testthing.cs.

You cannot understand testthing.cs.

You must respect testthing.cs.

It was here before you.

It will be here after you.

7. Making Games Means Learning EVERYTHING

This is probably my favorite part.

When I started making games, I thought game development meant programming.

Nope.

You want a game?

Cool.

Learn programming.

Now learn UI.

Now learn animation.

Now learn lighting.

Now learn sound.

Now learn level design.

Now learn optimization.

Now learn save systems.

Now learn dialogue systems.

Now learn shaders.

Now learn 3D modeling.

Now learn how cameras work.

Now learn why your shadows look like shit.

Now learn why your character falls through the floor at 400 miles per hour.

Now learn why everything runs perfectly in the editor but explodes in the build.

And even if you're not doing every single part yourself, you eventually need to understand enough about the other parts to make everything work together.

That's one reason I'm making Floodgate.

It's a real game that I intend to finish, but it's also my giant “learn how the hell 3D games work” experiment.

Every new system is another excuse to learn something.

Sometimes that means making something cool.

Sometimes that means staring at Unity at midnight wondering why Sebastian has achieved flight.

Both are educational experiences.

8. The Best Way I've Learned Programming Is By Needing Something

You can teach me what a system does.

That's useful.

But the moment I actually need that system for something I'm making?

Now I care.

I don't want to learn saving because today's lesson says:

SAVE DATA — CHAPTER 7

I want to learn saving because:

OH FUCK, MY GAME NEEDS TO REMEMBER THIS.

Suddenly the problem is real.

I have a reason to understand it.

That's how I've learned a ridiculous amount of programming.

I want something to happen.

I don't know how to make it happen.

So I figure it out.

Then I want something slightly more complicated.

So I figure that out.

Repeat this process for several years and eventually you look at something you've made and realize:

Wait.

I know how to do this now.

That's a really cool feeling.

9. Programming Is Mostly Problem Solving

Knowing syntax is useful.

Knowing every function in an engine is useful.

But I think one of the biggest skills is being able to take:

“I want this thing to happen.”

and break it into:

“Okay, what actually needs to happen?”

Let's say I want Sebastian to react when the player buys a flashlight upgrade.

Sounds simple.

But the computer doesn't understand:

“Sebastian reacts when you upgrade your flashlight.”

You have to decide what that actually means.

Did the player interact with the upgrade?

Do they have enough research?

What level is the flashlight currently?

Subtract the research.

Increase the flashlight level.

Change its properties.

Change the upgrade display.

Play Sebastian's dialogue.

Prevent the player from buying the same level twice.

Save the new level if necessary.

Suddenly one sentence became ten little problems.

And that's programming.

Not commanding a computer to MAKE COOL THING.

It's figuring out all the tiny boring things that must happen for the cool thing to exist.

10. Simple Is Usually Better

When you're learning, there's a temptation to make everything incredibly clever.

You could make this enormous universal system with inheritance and interfaces and seventeen layers of abstraction that dynamically handles every possible situation.

Or...

You could write twelve understandable lines of code.

I've increasingly become a fan of the second option.

Obviously good architecture matters, especially when projects get large.

But complicated doesn't automatically mean good.

If I can open a script six months later and understand what it's doing, that's worth a LOT.

Future me is an idiot.

I have to write code for him.

11. You Don't Have To Know Everything

This one took me a while.

Programming has this weird ability to make you feel stupid because there is always something you don't know.

You learn one language.

Someone knows three.

You make a game.

Someone made an engine.

You make an engine.

Some psychopath writes an operating system.

There is no finish line where the computer descends from heaven and announces:

CONGRATULATIONS.

YOU NOW KNOW PROGRAMMING.

You're always going to encounter something and think:

What the fuck is that?

And that's fine.

The actual useful skill is knowing how to approach something you don't understand.

Read.

Experiment.

Break it.

Figure out why it broke.

Fix it.

Break it differently.

Eventually understand it.

Then six months later forget half of it and look it up again.

12. Finishing Something Teaches You Different Things Than Starting Something

Starting games is awesome.

Everything is possibilities.

You make the movement.

You make the first room.

You add some mechanics.

You think:

THIS IS GONNA BE SO COOL.

Finishing games is where the bodies are buried.

Now you have to deal with menus.

Saving.

Edge cases.

Performance.

Settings.

Balancing.

Tutorials.

Polish.

Bugs.

More bugs.

Bugs caused by fixing bugs.

That one bug you swear you've fixed three times.

And all the tiny boring things that separate:

“Look at this cool thing I made.”

from:

“Here is a game you can actually play from beginning to end.”

That's part of why I want to finish Floodgate instead of letting it become another experiment I eventually abandon.

I want the experience of building the beginning.

And the middle.

And the end.

And then fixing the stupid shit between all three.

13. Nobody Knows What They're Doing Until They Do

I think programming looks like magic when you don't know how to do it.

You see a game running and think:

How the FUCK did somebody make this?

Then you start learning.

And eventually you realize the answer is usually:

They made one thing.

Then another thing.

Then another thing.

They got stuck.

They figured it out.

They made another thing.

They broke the first thing.

They fixed it.

Repeat.

A finished game can contain thousands upon thousands of tiny decisions, but nobody sat down on day one knowing the solution to all of them.

They just kept solving the next problem.

And I think that's probably the biggest thing making games has taught me.

You don't need to know how to make your entire dream game today.

You need to know how to make the next thing.

Right now, I'm learning 3D development.

I'm making rooms.

Programming systems.

Figuring out lighting.

Making shops.

Breaking things.

Fixing things.

Breaking them again.

Someday I want to make games far bigger than what I can make right now.

I don't know how to make those games yet.

That's okay.

That's why I'm making the ones I can make.

Anyway

School can teach you programming.

And I'm glad it can.

But actually making things has taught me a completely different side of it.

Programming isn't sitting in a dark room furiously typing incomprehensible green text into a terminal while yelling:

“I'M IN.”

It's mostly:

“Why isn't this working?”

followed by:

“Oh.”

followed by:

“I'm an idiot.”

followed by:

“HOLY SHIT IT WORKS.”

And then, approximately ten minutes later:

“Why isn't this working?”

Again.

Forever.

And honestly?

I fucking love it.
